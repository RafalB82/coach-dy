# Coach DY — Evidence Base

Status: 2026-11-08. Purpose: separate what is scientifically supported in Dorian Yates' method from what is practice, observation, or an n=1 case. This file does not replace medical knowledge.

Legend:
- **Supported** — consistent evidence (meta-analysis, RCT, review) supports the direction.
- **Partly supported** — mixed evidence or context-dependent (population, training age, goal).
- **Not supported** — no evidence of superiority; it may work, but it is not optimal.
- **Contradicted** — evidence points the other way.

## 1. Resistance training

| Dorian's claim | Key evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 2–3 min rest between sets | Schoenfeld 2016: 3 min > 1 min for hypertrophy and strength in trained lifters | **Supported** |
| 2×/week is enough for the "average person" | Schoenfeld 2016: 2× > 1× at equal volume; Schoenfeld 2019: at equal volume, 1–3× show no difference | **Supported as a minimum** |
| Training to failure / close to failure | Grgic 2021: no overall difference, but an advantage for hypertrophy in trained lifters (ES 0.15); Robinson 2024: hypertrophy increases closer to failure | **Partly supported** |
| One set to failure is optimal | Krieger 2010: multiple sets +40% hypertrophy vs one set; Schoenfeld 2017: more sets → more hypertrophy | **Not supported as optimal** |
| Low volume (HIT) is sufficient | Giessing 2016: HIT 2×/week produced greater strength in 3/9 exercises and similar body composition, with ~10.5 min sessions vs 24–27 min | **Partly / context-dependent** |
| Progressive overload | The entire RT literature; no single "breakthrough" study | **Supported** |
| 5–6 weeks hard + 2 weeks light | Moesgaard 2022: periodization improves strength, not hypertrophy, at equal volume; Coleman 2024: deload does not harm hypertrophy, but continuous training is better for strength | **Partly supported** |
| Each muscle group 1×/week | Schoenfeld 2019: at equal volume, frequency does not differentiate hypertrophy | **Partly supported** — works if weekly volume is adequate |
| ~45 min session | Giessing 2016: HIT sessions ~10–27 min; health guidelines | **Supported as sufficient for health** |
| Mind-muscle connection before failure | Research on internal focus: mixed, mostly EMG, not hypertrophy | **Weak / partly supported** |
| "Pump ≠ growth" | The pump is mainly blood flow; hypertrophy mechanisms are mechanical tension and metabolic stress | **Directionally supported**, but the simple binary "the pump doesn't matter" is an oversimplification |

## 2. Cardio, diet, recovery

| Dorian's claim | Key evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 6 min HIIT ≈ 45 min steady cardio | Gillen 2016: 3×20 s all-out, 3×/week, 10-min session vs 45 min MICT, 12 weeks — similar VO2max (+19%), insulin sensitivity, mitochondria | **Supported** for health/conditioning |
| Cardio separate from strength training | Concurrent-training literature: interference is small, larger with high cardio volume and poor nutrition | **Partly supported** |
| Low-carb diet for fatty liver | 2026 RCT (Washington University): keto −67% liver fat vs −45% low-fat; 2022 review: all calorie-deficit diets work | **Partly supported** |
| Protein ~1 g/lb | Morton 2018: plateau ~1.62 g/kg/day (~0.73 g/lb) | **Not supported as necessary**; 1 g/lb is an upper bound, not a threshold |
| ~1% muscle loss/year after 40 | Sarcopenia reviews: ~1%/year from middle age, 3–8%/decade after 40 | **Supported** |
| Deload / week off 1–2×/year | Coleman 2024: 1-week deload does not harm hypertrophy; it may reduce strength gains vs continuous training | **Partly supported** |

## 4. Context: why HIT is rational for people with work, children, and limited recovery

This is the most important practical argument for Dorian's approach — and it is stronger than the argument "best hypertrophy method".

**a) Limited time.** A HIT session 2×/week for 30–45 min (and in Giessing's study even ~10–27 min) leaves time for work, family, and cardio. The alternative of "6 days a week for 90 min" is unsustainable for most people. Adherence is part of effectiveness: the best program is the one you actually do.

**b) Limited recovery.** With work, children, poor sleep, stress, and age, recovery is the bottleneck. Low volume reduces weekly fatigue and preserves set quality. This does not mean "the less, the better" — it means **volume should match recovery**, not be maximized at all costs.

**c) Age.** Older people recover more slowly, but they still need enough volume to counteract sarcopenia. Practical conclusion: start with 2×/week full body, 1–2 sets per exercise, 8–10 exercises, cautious progression; increase volume only when recovery and results allow. Do not confuse "low volume" with "no volume".

**d) Health goal vs maximal hypertrophy.** For health, strength, functionality, and a "good enough" physique, HIT is a rational practical optimum. For maximal hypertrophy in a well-recovered, advanced trainee with a large budget of time and recovery — higher volume usually wins.

**e) Definition of "optimal".** Optimal = best under given constraints (time, sleep, stress, age, adherence), not best in a lab with unlimited time and recovery. In that sense, Dorian's approach **is optimal for a large share of people** — those who are not professional bodybuilders.

## 5. The Dorian n=1 argument

**What supports it:**
- 6× Mr. Olympia (1992–1997) — proof that his method can produce elite hypertrophy in him.
- At 63–64 he is active, healthy, pain-free, with a good quality of life — this supports the claim that his **current** lifestyle (not necessarily the 1990s extreme) is sustainable. Note: this is a statement/public image from the podcast, not an independent medical examination.
- Decades of practice, a training log, consistency — this is not internet theory.

**What limits the inference:**
- n=1, genetics, winner's selection (survivorship bias).
- Anabolic steroids during his professional career — they accelerate recovery and change the response to volume; Dorian himself talks about the difference between natural and "on gear".
- His current training is not the same as Olympia preparation: currently 1× upper / 1× lower, light weights, Pilates, functional training, plenty of cardio.
- "Healthy" does not prove the method is optimal for everyone — it proves that it **is feasible and can be safe** with sensible management.

Conclusion: Dorian is a **strong case study and a source of principles**, but not proof of universal optimality. We combine his practice with evidence and the user's context.

## 6. How to formulate recommendations (decision rules)

- **Goal: health, strength, time, limited recovery** → HIT/low volume: 2×/week, 8–10 exercises, 1–2 sets to 0–2 RIR, 2–3 min rest, progression. A good default.
- **Goal: maximal hypertrophy, good sleep, advanced training age** → gradually increase volume toward 10–20 sets/muscle/week (depending on tolerance), keep 0–2 RIR, 2–3 min rest, deload when performance drops.
- **Older/new trainee** → start low, learn technique, 2×/week, add volume slowly; monitor pain, sleep, recovery.
- **Pain/injury/illness** → escalate to a specialist; do not diagnose.
- **Never claim** that "one set is optimal for everyone" or that "low volume beats high volume" as a universal rule.

## 7. Gaps and interpretive risks

- Most studies last 6–12 weeks, involve untrained/recreational people, and use indirect measures of hypertrophy (BIA, ultrasound).
- Elite bodybuilders are underrepresented; interventions with them are usually n=1 or small case series.
- Anabolic steroids are a confounder for recovery and hypertrophy.
- Definitions of "failure" and "RIR" vary across studies.
- Large individual variability in the response to volume; the "average" may not fit the individual.
- Do not use evidence to justify unsafe practices (extreme dehydration, doping, extreme deficits).
